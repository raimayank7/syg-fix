Ohm's law explained with examples

One equation connects the three basic quantities of a circuit.

The three quantities

  • Voltage (V) is the push, measured in volts. It exists between two points.
  • Current (I) is the flow, measured in amperes (amps). It flows through a part.
  • Resistance (R) is the opposition to the flow, measured in ohms.

A water pipe is a common picture: voltage is the pressure, current is the amount of water flowing per second, and resistance is how narrow the pipe is.

The law

V = I × R. Turned around: I = V / R and R = V / I.

Power

Power is the rate at which energy is turned into heat or light: P = V × I. Using Ohm's law this is also I² × R and V² / R. The heat is why a resistor has a power rating.

Units and prefixes

1 kΩ is 1,000 Ω, 1 mA is 0.001 A and 1 µF is 0.000001 F. Writing a current in milliamps in a formula that needs amps is the most common mistake: 20 mA is 0.02 A. The calculators accept 20m.

Limits

Ohm's law describes parts whose resistance stays constant, such as resistors. An LED or a diode does not follow it: its voltage barely changes with current, which is why it needs a resistor in series.

Is it V = IR or V = I/R?

V = I × R. A higher resistance needs more voltage for the same current.

Does it work with AC?

For resistors with RMS values, yes.
