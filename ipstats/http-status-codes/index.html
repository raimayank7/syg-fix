HTTP status codes: what 404, 403, 500 and the rest mean

Every time a browser asks for a page, the server answers with a three-digit code. Type a number or word to find what it means.

How the codes work

Before sending a page, a web server sends a status code that says how the request went. You never see it when all is well (the code is 200). You see it when something fails, usually as "404 Not Found" or "503 Service Unavailable" in large letters.

The first digit tells you the family, and that is most of what you need:

  • 1xx: wait. The server is still working on it. Rarely seen.
  • 2xx: success. The request worked.
  • 3xx: look elsewhere. The thing has moved, or your saved copy is still good. The browser follows these by itself.
  • 4xx: the request was the problem. A wrong address, a missing login, no permission, or too many requests. Something on the asking side needs to change.
  • 5xx: the server is the problem. The website broke or is overloaded. Nothing you do on your device will fix it; waiting usually does.

So the quickest reading of any error is its first digit: a 4 means check the address, your login or your permissions; a 5 means the site has a fault and you should try again later.

Nothing matches. Try a shorter word or just the number.

What to do about the ones you meet most

404 Not Found. Check the address for typing slips. Go to the site's home page and search for the page from there. If you followed a link from another site, the page has probably been moved or removed.

403 Forbidden. You are not allowed to see the page. Log in if the site has accounts. If you are sure you should have access, a VPN, an office network or a shared address may have been blocked; try another connection.

401 Unauthorized. Log in again. A wrong password or an expired session causes it.

429 Too Many Requests. Stop reloading and wait a few minutes. If you share your public address with many people (see carrier-grade NAT), someone else may have used up the allowance.

400 Bad Request. Clear the cookies for that one site and try again. A damaged or oversized cookie is the usual cause.

500 Internal Server Error. The website's own software failed. Reload once after a minute. If it continues, only the site's owners can fix it.

502 Bad Gateway and 504 Gateway Timeout. Large sites put a front server before their real servers. These codes mean the front server could not get a good answer in time. They usually clear within minutes.

503 Service Unavailable. The site is overloaded or under maintenance. Popular sites show it during ticket sales, results days and sales events. Try again a little later rather than hammering the reload button, which adds to the load.

Errors that are not status codes

Messages such as "This site can't be reached", "DNS address could not be found" or "Connection timed out" come from your own browser. They mean no answer arrived at all, so there is no code. The cause is usually your connection, DNS, or a server that is completely down. Our slow Wi-Fi fixes and DNS guide cover these.

Is a 404 error a problem with my internet?

No. A 404 came from the website's server, so your connection is working. The page simply is not at that address.

What is the difference between 401 and 403?

401 means "tell me who you are first". 403 means "I know enough, and the answer is no".

What is the difference between 301 and 302?

Both send you to another address. 301 says the move is permanent; 302 says it is for now.
